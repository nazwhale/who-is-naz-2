> Originally published in in Canvas Online Magazine, 2022

It is said that simplicity is the nature of great souls. Think the prose of George Orwell and the wisdom of Alan Watts, Lou Reed’s ‘Perfect Day’ and Dylan’s ‘Blowin’ in the Wind’. Indeed, at first glance, some things seem so brilliant and simple that we wonder how it is we didn’t write, think or sing them first.

So it is fitting that Ted Lucas, a virtuosic session musician, should produce perhaps the most straightforwardly beautiful album I’ve ever heard.

You’ve felt his vibrations. Once holder of dream-job-title ‘exotic string specialist for Motown records’, he contributed various instruments to tracks for The Temptations, The Supremes and Stevie Wonder. He’s also opened for the Eagles, Frank Zappa and Black Sabbath and strummed the sitar under the guidance of Harihar Rao and Ravi Shankar. Despite this, the most notice his solo compositions have received are thanks to Erica Bjuremark’s sweet but forgettable cover of ‘It’s So Easy’ for a polished commercial by Audi. After a career spent playing with the stars Ted Lucas got lost in space, passing in 1992 and leaving only the faintest of traces in music history.

However, the recognition that has eluded him for so long (possibly because he didn’t give a hoot in the first place) is slowly arriving. Sometime last year Ted Lucas’ lone solo LP (nicknamed ‘The OM Album’) was reissued on vinyl by Yoga Records.

The winged scarab beetle on the cover, designed by psychedelic rock artist Stanley Mouse, was originally intended for Jimi Hendrix’s ‘First Rays of the New Rising Sun’. Hendrix died before the album was finished and the beetle scuttled over to Ted. According to the reissue’s liner notes, Mouse was unhappy, uttering the unforgettable: "Ted hijacked Jimi Hendrix's album cover and stole my green socks."

Only 1000 copies were ever pressed and the album was unheard outside Detroit, largely due to its getting lost in the waves of earnest-confessional singer-songwriters flooding America in the early 1970s. Mostly recorded in Lucas’ attic, it has a deeply personal feel, crystallising simple truths, modestly tapping into universal themes of ordinary love, feeling low and getting high.

Evoking John Fahey and Nick Drake, it’s an enchanting thing of simple beauty. The album can be split into two clear halves. The first comprises six flawless psych-folk gems, the second a couple of improvised guitar instrumentals and an extended blues. Whilst the instrumentals are impressive, the real magic is found in the understated charm of songs like ‘It’s So Easy’, ‘Plain & Sane & Simple Melody’, and ‘It Is So Nice to Get Stoned’ – a song that wins listens on the title alone. ‘Baby Where You Are’ evokes early ‘60s Dylan, elegantly complemented by Lucas’ mellow lines. ‘I’ll Find a Way (To Carry It All)’ is a bittersweet pondering on soured love, set against a gorgeous background of ethereal flickering strings. Light melodies weave their way between your neurons. Acoustics sooth. Lucas coos.

It’s immediately resonant music to drift off to. An album of sensations akin to the simple pleasures of floating on the sea, lying in long grass and feeling the wind through your hair. Lucas’ affection for the purity of Indian ragas is knitted into the fabric of the album. He goes for earthy sounds and stays clear of technology. Lyrics have no ties to pop culture or any notion of ‘cool’, instead conveying thoughts and feelings that every one of us can relate to (‘it is so nice to lay down/and watch the clouds drifting so lazily’). Therein lies its timelessness.

A love of the natural was central to Ted’s philosophy on life (In a rare interview on late night Detroit radio Lucas summarises this in a passionate rant about his disgust for neon lights; “its unnatural…that’s what’s driving people bananas!”). He comes across a warm, rambling champion of creativity, clearly disheartened with the material world and the sacrifices of quality made for costs. 

Music is the most profound form of communication we as humans have created on this planet.

In a recent conversation with his son, Peter, Ted’s perfectionism reverberates. One anecdote tells of the first pressing of the OM Album, in which the record company used a particular kind of plastic to try and save money, printed with a yellow (“lemon”) label. Ted was infuriated, and made them repress the record with a higher quality material, this time with a pink label. His son recalls how, ten years later, when looking for a copy for a friend, Ted kept pulling out lemons to his great displeasure. He continued to smash two boxes of records until he finally landed on a pink one. “It felt like it was raining vinyl”, his son recalls. Consequently the lemons, though inferior in quality, are the rarest version of the OM Album around.

Eclectic music lover and sonic geek, Ted’s record collection contained such oddities as Balinese Gamboan music and Buddhist chants amongst the blues of Arthur Big Boy Cudrup and Howlin’ Wolf, Indian Ragas and classical guitar. A small amount of Ted’s own material was found amongst his mountains of records - mostly snippets and outtakes, unfinished demos and a couple of rough cuts for a lost second album. However his commitment to Motown came first. This, in addition to family problems and the relative flop of the OM album, meant his additional personal output was never formalised. We’re left today with but a single nugget of loner folk to treasure.

The liner notes end: ‘there aren't many of us left to tell you about Ted and his music. You'll have to discover that for yourself’.

Well, here ya go.

Further Listening

Ted Lucas (‘The OM Album’) is now available on iTunes and YouTube (still no Spotify to date).
Lucas served as a member of some psychedelic folk/rock bands with cult followings. Try the sounds of the Spike Drivers, the Misty Wizards and the Horny Toads.
