I read a quote by Mark Twain recently. I know everyone quotes Mark Twain, but this is a good’un:

“The more people I meet, the better I like my dog”

In certain moods, this neatly sums up how I feel about Jonathan Richman and a lot of the sounds I’ve heard lately. Im not here to make any wild statements on the current state of music – that’s a different article. But I’ll concede that there are words I’d use to describle the music of JR that I can’t see myself bestowing on any other artist around now. His music overflows with affection, humility, honesty, romanticism and a total lack of ego. There’s no frontin’ here. In a world of self-proclaimed idols shouting over each other about having the dopest flow or the biggest butt the net result is an urge to stick my fingers in my ears, close my eyes and wish for something real, something human. Yes, music can be a transportive art; Waits sends you to burnt out whiskey bars, Kanye to bling Bel Air mansions and Bowie can shoot you into space. But it can also bring you back down to earth – illuminating the self through the prism of another human’s perspective. 

And check out that smile!

In the age of anxiety, it seems more and more of us can relate to the expirience of hiding one’s soul. Schoolteachers pounce upon our mistakes from kindergarten. Individual differences are ridiculed. Quirks are ironed out and we’re flung into the world scared to death of being judged or rejected. 

Endless milling through social media feeds painful awareness of the dissonance between public persona and the inner world. Staring at one’s Facebook, LinkedIn or whatever it seems fitting to ask, “is this really me? Do I not amount to something more than Naz, 22, who just ate a crumpet?” Unfortunately Facebook has no box for “intangible human spirit”. It’s neglecting the only real thing about us. And so we forget it in ourselves.

In an inspiring letter to his son (very much worth absorbing in its entirety), Ted Hughes mused on the inner child, the vessel for our human spirit, and its innate longing to be liberated from the suffocation of modern social convention.

“Everybody tries to protect this [child] inside, and to acquire skills and aptitudes for dealing with the situations that threaten to overwhelm it. So everybody develops a whole armour of secondary self, the artificially constructed being that deals with the outer world.

Usually, that child is a wretchedly isolated undeveloped little being. It’s been protected by the efficient armour, it’s never participated in life…And it’s never properly lived.

That little creature is sitting there, behind the armour, peering through the slits. That’s the carrier of all the living qualities. It’s the centre of all the possible magic and revelation.”

The child wants to be heard, but it is quiet. How can we appeal to this tiny mystic being and break past Hughes’ ‘second self’?

Allow me to offer a suggestion:

Jonathan Richman was born in Natick, Massachusetts, the kind of humdrum town that makes you want to run away to somewhere like New York City. Aged 18, he did just that, intoxicated by the dark shimmer of the apple. An obsession with the music of the Velvet Underground and a dash of luck found young Jonathan crashing on the band manager’s couch. It’s commonly quoted that the first Velvet Underground album may have only sold 30,000 copies, but everyone who bought one started a band. Jonathan attended over 100 of their shows, turning up with scribbled poems presented to his idols. Music was in his veins. It was only a matter of time before the stuff started flowing out of him.

The sound of his band on their first and last album, the epynomous ‘The Modern Lovers’, is intrinsically bound to the abrasive instrumentation and unconventional song structuires of the Velvet Underground. But instead of conjouring images of black leather and methamphetamines, he went for simple pleasures and honesty - a model example of turning influence into strikingly original output. ‘Roadrunner’ is pinned by a throbbing two-chord Velvet Underground rhythm straight out of ‘Sister Ray’, and turns a story of debauchery and grit into an impassioned love song to the freedom of driving fast with your favorite music on. It’s rock and roll at its direct relatable best. There’s zero bullshit and the result is magic. The song has been retrospectively viewed a protopunk masterpiece - Sex Pistols vocalist Johnny Rotten once said that whilst he “hates all music”, “Roadrunner” is his favorite song. 

Why the fuss? The lyrics are hardly poetic, and the instrumentation nothing new. But that vocal performance. 

(At this point, I emplore you to press play. Ideally, turn it up loud and stand in an empty room, naked, ready to shake it)
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6ZWoJ8_75Mo

He sounds like a kid in a world of his own, in verbal freefall, realeasing whatever words appear to him. Lines are repeated. Words are stumbled. It’s off key. But take a listen and feel that wild spirit inside you spring up. You can almost hear him dancing as he sings. And you want to join in. You don’t know why it feels so good, it just does. 

I believe we’re honing in on what Tolstoy broadly defines as art. Namely a transmission of “emotional infectiousness”:

“A real work of art destroys, in the consciousness of the receiver, the seperation between himself and the artist”

If you’re tapping along, I’d hazard to guess that the seperation of consciousness between you and Jonathan is melting away as you listen. Let the music obliterate. Dance. Fuse consciousness with a guy from Minnesota letting it all hang out nearly half a century ago. Time and place have been transcended – but the feeling is indentical. In all sincerity, is it not incredible? No doubt Leo would have got his freak on.  

The rest of the album finds Richman writing about loving his parents, not having sex and staying away from drugs. “I’m Straight” is a direct attack on hippies choosing to dumb their senses rather than engage with the world around them whilst “Old World” gives an impassioned and fond account of the 50s he grew up in. In the context of early 1970s NYC this was as risky and defiant as it got. What could be less cool? (Incedentally, what could be more punk?). Honesty is what strikes. The armour is down. We are eavesdropping on the inner child at play.

Seemingly unconcerned with the success and influence of ‘The Modern Lovers’, Jonathan embarked on the first of a whopping 25 solo albums.

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x_jRDVStzOY

It’s mostly just him and his guitar, occasionally accompanied by stripped-down drums. The impression is a shedding of anything inessential to the feeling of the song, and the result can be profoundly emotional. ‘That Summer Feeling’ evokes melancholy nostalgia for the freedom and simplicity of teenage summers with heartaching tenderness. That forgotten feeling of letting the inner child run riot for months on end.

“When there's things to do not because you gotta
When you run for love not because you oughta
When you trust your friends with no reason notta
The joy I've named shall not be tamed
And that summer feeling’s gonna haunt you one day in your life”

His albums are peppered with songs so juvinile to be laughable. “Chewing Gum Rapper”, “Double Chocolate Malted” and “Im a Little Dinosaur” could all concievably be written for primary school sing-a-longs. Live footage shows him getting down on all fours mid-performance and growling at the crowd. It all seems a bit ridiculous. But try to let go and smile. Sing to the ding-dings and the sha-la-las. Childish should not be extended to naïve. These songs are not stupid. They are fun, and if you wont admit it you’re probably peering through a suit of armour.

Ted Hughes completes his fatherly advice by imploring, from an old man to a young one, to shake off one’s inhibitions and be led by the inner child: 

“The only thing people regret is that they didn’t live boldly enough, that they didn’t invest enough heart, didn’t love enough. 
Nothing else really counts at all.”

Now who facies a boogie?
