>>> There was an old, crazy dude who used to live a long time ago. His name was Lord Buckley. And he said, a long time ago, he said, 'People... they're kinda like flowers...'” – Robin Williams

The reason I first came into contact with His Lordship is doubly
embarrassing. I’d googled my own name and spelt it wrong. Slipping
down a Wikipedia rabbit hole, I eventually surfaced at “The Nazz”, a monologue about Jesus by a man by the name of Richard Merle Buckley. I listened to it, the whole 10 minutes.

Genius is an overused word. Flabbergasting isn’t. It was flabbergasting.

Goofin’ off down the boulevard that intersects stand-up comedy, avant-
garde beat poetry and bebop preaching, there’s Lord Buckley, 6ft
6inches, sporting a Dalí moustache, tuxedo and rhinestone-studded
cape.

His medium was the monologue, using his ‘hipsemantic’ to riff on
legendary tales, classic literature and accounts of his own escapades.
Choice specimens include ‘The Nazz’ (Jesus of Nazareth, the
‘carpenter kitty’), ‘The Hip Gahn’ (an incident involving Gandhi), ‘The
Raven’ (by Edgar Allan Poe), ‘My Own Railroad’ (the time he drove his
car into the trolley car tracks in Chicago) and the darker ‘Bad Rapping
of the Marquis de Sade’.

“He was playing stories like an instrument” Arlo Guthrie

Lord Buckley’s catalogue is a treasure-trove for any lover of words and
wit. He christened his lingo ‘hipsemantic’, a fusion of the stiff vernacular
of British aristocracy and the loose jazz slang of black America:
“Negroes spoke a language of such power, purity and beauty”, Buckley
wrote, “I could not resist this magical way of speaking…it triple-hipped
my soul”.

Here’s Buckley on primetime in 1958. Skip to 5.20 to hear an inch-and-
a-half of his jive-retelling of Shakespeare’s “Friends, Romans,
countrymen, lend me your ears”.

“[He’s] a secret thing that people pass under the table…there are a lot
of people influenced by Lord Buckley who have never heard his
material” Ken Kesey

Until the end Buckley’s audiences remained small and his records
obscure – it seemed he was appreciated more by performers than the
public. Quincy Jones and Groucho Marx were admirers of his, as was
Frank Sinatra, until His Lordship marched sixteen naked people through
the lobby of the Royal Hawaiian Hotel where he was performing. In the
subsequent decades, his inspiration lived on.

Young Robert Zimmerman was a huge fan - Dylanologists will have
spotted Buckley’s album on the cover of ‘Bringing It All Back Home’,
sitting above the fireplace. Additionally, the ‘jingle-jangle morning’ of ‘Mr
Tambourine Man’ was lifted from The Lord’s performance of ‘Digital
Scrooge’.

Similarly, Buckley was an idol of Tom Waits, who worked with one
composer chiefly because he had previously worked with The Lord:
“Tom was just floored and started asking me questions. Eventually he
just said, ‘well if you knew him, we can work together’”.

More recently, Jaylib (J-Dilla x Madlib) sampled ‘Scrooge’ in ‘Louder’
and shape-shifting gorilla Damon Albarn took a slice of ‘The Gasser’ for
his single ‘Everyday Robots’.

In the autumn of 1960 Buckley was set to play a number of New York
club nights and appear on The Ed Sullivan Show. However, a run-in
with the vice-squad resulted in the confiscation of his cabaret card on

the premise of an arrest for public drunkenness some 20 years earlier.
A committee of performers came to his defence, contesting that the
cards perpetuated a culture of bribery and corruption. The accusation
made front page news and led to the abolition of cabaret cards years
later. Tragically, this was too late for The Lord.

Attempts to get the card reinstated flopped. With no way to earn a living,
Buckley sunk into depression. A month later he called friend Harold
Humes, explaining he was broke, hungry and afraid. That same evening
The Lord’s curtain came down. The physicians said the cause of death
was stroke caused by “extreme hypertension”. A friend later commented
that, in essence, he had died of a broken heart. Another took a different
line, “Lord Buckley was so heavy, he just fell off the planet”.

Though His Lordship’s key themes were respect, kindness and love, he
was not a religious man. In the veins of his most celebrated work, “The
Nazz”, is the strikingly modern philosophy that if anything is to be
celebrated and worshipped, it is the beauty of human beings.

And so goes the epilogue to his 1951 LP “Euphoria”:

&quot;People! Yes, people are the true flowers of life, and it has been a most
precious pleasure to have temporarily strolled in your garden.&quot;
